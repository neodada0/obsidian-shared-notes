Methods

Study Design

This prospective, single-arm, repeated-measures intervention study included an embedded quantitative process evaluation. During the planned 6-week intervention, SoundBridge supported home practice and collaboration among children, caregivers, and speech-language pathologists alongside routine face-to-face speech therapy in South Korea. It also generated time-stamped records of their platform-mediated activities for the process evaluation.

Speech assessments and questionnaires were scheduled at baseline (T0), midpoint (T1), and postintervention (T2), as applicable. Speech recordings were scored by raters blinded to child identity and assessment time point.

The approved protocol specified a 2-arm randomized trial. Because a comparison group could not be recruited, randomization was not implemented. Participants were enrolled from June 1 to July 3, 2026. The study was retrospectively registered with the Clinical Research Information Service on September 17, 2026 (KCT0012640).

Reporting was guided by the TREND statement [1] (Multimedia Appendix 1), and the intervention description was structured with reference to the TIDieR checklist [2] (Multimedia Appendix 2).

Setting and Participants

Child–caregiver–SLP triads were recruited from routine pediatric speech-language therapy settings in South Korea. Each triad included a child, their primary caregiver, and their treating SLP; caregivers and SLPs could participate in multiple triads.

At enrollment, children were eligible if they were aged 4–7 years; had persistent errors in velar stops, alveolar fricatives, or affricates on standardized assessment or according to their treating SLP’s clinical judgment; received face-to-face therapy at least weekly; had completed at least 3 sessions with that SLP, including assessment, and established rapport; and could perform basic study tasks and use a digital device with caregiver support without aversion or hypersensitivity. Children were excluded for a bilingual language environment or nonnative Korean; an organic speech sound disorder; visual or hearing difficulties that precluded audiovisual materials; or a severe co-occurring condition that substantially limited task performance or the ability to follow instructions. Co-occurring language delay alone did not exclude participation.

Caregivers had to be the child’s primary caregiver, able to consult regularly with the SLP and support home practice, and able to use SoundBridge on a smartphone. They were excluded for insufficient Korean literacy to use the service, communicate with the research team, or complete questionnaires; anticipated difficulty maintaining the child’s at least weekly face-to-face therapy; or plans to discontinue it during the study. SLPs needed a Korean national level 1 or 2 speech-language pathology credential, at least 1 year of experience treating children with speech sound disorders, and at least 1 eligible child in their care. They also had to be able to integrate SoundBridge into practice without aversion and expect to continue intervention delivery and study participation throughout the study.

SLPs were recruited through notices at pediatric speech-language therapy institutions and through professional, academic, and online networks. They gave caregivers of potentially eligible children in their caseloads a research-team-provided notice stating that declining would not affect ongoing treatment. SLPs and caregivers completed online screening questionnaires; triads were enrolled after eligibility, consent, and child assent were confirmed.

Sample Size

The target of 30 children (30 child–caregiver–SLP triads) was set with reference to Hertzog’s example of a single-group repeated-measures design [27], together with the study timeline, budget, and feasibility of recruiting triads. In that example, 30 participants provided an estimated power of .80 to detect a time effect of η²=.08 with 3 measurements, assuming α=.05, a within-participant correlation of .40, and a sphericity correction of ε=.80.

Pilot Testing

Two speech-language pathology faculty members and one clinical expert reviewed SoundBridge and the study procedures for clinical appropriateness and feasibility. A 3-week pilot with one SLP and two child–caregiver dyads followed the planned procedures except for T1 assessment; its participants and data were excluded from the main study. The pilot led to optional rerecording after children listened to and evaluated their productions, a simpler interface and instructions to reduce unintended selections and incomplete responses, and supplemental caregiver-assisted recording because Zoom audio quality varied across devices.

SoundBridge Intervention

SoundBridge supplemented routine face-to-face speech therapy during a planned 6-week intervention. The closed, role-based service was accessible only to enrolled participants and comprised SoundBridge Play, a mobile web module for children and caregivers, and SoundBridge Connect, a web module for SLPs. In Play, children said each target word 3 times consecutively; the 3 productions in one attempt were stored as a single recording. They then listened to their recording and the model audio, judged whether their production matched the target, and chose whether to record again. Caregivers were instructed not to guide this self-evaluation. In a separate step, they rated each recording as correct or incorrect, provided feedback and praise, and could request feedback from the SLP. They were instructed to rate a recording as correct if at least 2 of its 3 repetitions were accurate. In Connect, SLPs assigned home-practice tasks; reviewed recordings, child self-evaluations, caregiver ratings and questions, and accumulated practice records; and sent praise and feedback. Platform activities were stored as structured, time-stamped records.

SLPs were asked to create a home-practice task after a face-to-face session each week, specifying the target sound, word position, and syllable count. Each task was to contain at least 10 words in a 1:1 ratio of words practiced in that session to words with the same target sound that had not been practiced in the session. Targets were sounds the child could produce accurately in the clinic but needed to generalize. SLPs also received guidance to provide at least 15 minutes of articulation treatment with at least 30 target-sound productions and at least 5 minutes of caregiver counseling and coaching per face-to-face session; monitor home practice and send the child praise at least weekly; and respond to caregiver feedback requests as needed. These were instructions, not evidence that every session met them. SLPs retained clinical discretion and documented treatment using an activity checklist. Attended and missed sessions and articulation-treatment minutes were recorded.

Home practice was recommended once daily for approximately 10–15 minutes on 3 nontherapy days per week, alongside face-to-face therapy at least weekly. Before the intervention began, caregivers and SLPs separately received a 30-minute videoconference orientation to their respective functions and study procedures. On the intervention start day, each SLP–caregiver pair agreed on the intervention goals, target sounds, task difficulty, and initial home-practice task. Technical and procedural support was available throughout the intervention.

Outcome Measures

Existing questionnaires were adapted and study-specific items were developed for the study’s measurement aims and pediatric speech intervention context. The same 3-member speech-language pathology panel described under Pilot Testing (2 faculty members and 1 clinical expert) reviewed new and adapted items for construct relevance, clarity, and suitability for the intended respondents and context. Panel comments and pilot feedback informed revisions to item wording and response presentation. Internal consistency of multi-item scales was assessed using Cronbach α separately by informant and time point. Additional details on the assessment schedule, measures, study-developed items, probe procedures, and reliability are provided in Multimedia Appendix 3.

Primary Outcome

The primary outcome was the change from T0 to T2 in total percentage of consonants correct (PCC) on the multisyllabic word subtest of the Korean Articulation Phonology Profile (K-APP). The subtest contains 32 items and 91 consonant opportunities per assessment [9]. Target and nontarget PCC for the target-specificity analysis were derived from the same items.

Secondary Outcomes

Secondary speech outcomes were target percentage of consonants correct (PCC) on a study-developed speech generalization probe and caregiver-rated speech intelligibility. The probe was designed to examine generalization of target speech sounds to untrained lexical items at the word, phrase, and sentence levels. The probe comprised 3 sets targeting Korean velar stops, alveolar fricatives, and affricates. At T0, T1, and T2, each child completed the set for the sound class containing their treatment target or targets. Two level 1 SLPs, each with more than 10 years of clinical experience, reviewed the probe items. Probe words were not used for treatment or practice in face-to-face therapy or SoundBridge. Target PCC was the percentage of consonants from the assigned sound class produced correctly across the set. Nontarget PCC was calculated from the remaining consonants in the same items for the target-specificity analysis.

Speech intelligibility was measured at T0, T1, and T2 using the Korean Intelligibility in Context Scale (ICS-K) [10,11]. Caregivers rated how well different communication partners understood the child on a 5-point scale. The 7-item mean was calculated, with higher scores indicating greater intelligibility.

Secondary process outcomes were perceived child self-monitoring, therapeutic alliance, and self-efficacy. Perceived self-monitoring was assessed with 14 items developed from behaviors described by Shriberg and Kwiatkowski [3], with 2 items in each of 7 domains: recognition, preplanning, elicited self-correction, prompted self-correction, self-evaluation, spontaneous self-correction, and overgeneralization. Caregivers and treating SLPs rated the child separately on a 5-point scale at T0, T1, and T2. The 2 overgeneralization items were reverse-scored before calculating the 14-item mean; higher scores indicated more adaptive perceived self-monitoring.

Caregiver–SLP working alliance was assessed at T0, T1, and T2 using adapted 12-item client and therapist versions of the Working Alliance Inventory–Short Revised [4-6]. Caregivers and SLPs completed their respective versions on a 5-point scale, and scores were calculated separately as the mean of 12 items. Child–SLP alliance was assessed exploratorily at the same time points using 4 items selected from the Therapeutic Alliance Scale for Children–Revised and adapted for the children’s age and study context [7,8]. Children selected their own responses on a 4-point scale presented with facial-expression pictures; the 4-item mean was used.

Self-efficacy was assessed at T0 and T2. Caregiver self-efficacy for home intervention used 15 items from the parent domain of the Korean Parenting Efficacy Test (K-PET), adapted for supporting the child’s speech practice [12]. Responses were rated on a 5-point scale, items 9–15 were reverse-scored, and the item mean was calculated. SLP self-efficacy for caregiver counseling used 14 items adapted from the Counselor Activity Self-Efficacy Scales (CASES) and 2 new items on coaching caregivers to sustain motivation for home practice and resolve difficulties [13,14]. SLPs completed the 16 items on a 5-point scale for each participating child; the item mean was used. Child speech self-efficacy used 3 study-developed items on perceived speech performance, self-monitoring, and social communication. Children responded on a 4-point scale, and the 3-item mean was treated as an exploratory index. Higher scores indicated greater self-efficacy on all 3 measures.

Baseline Measures

At baseline (T0), age was calculated in months, nonverbal cognitive ability was assessed using the Korean Colored Progressive Matrices (K-CPM) [15,16], and speech severity was indexed by K-APP total PCC. Treating SLPs provided receptive and expressive vocabulary scores from the Receptive and Expressive Vocabulary Test (REVT) [17-19], administered during routine clinical care.

Usability and Acceptability Measures

Usability, perceived usefulness, satisfaction, intention to use, and willingness to recommend were evaluated at T2. Caregivers and SLPs rated SoundBridge usability using a 10-item, positively worded version of the System Usability Scale (SUS) [20-22]. Items were rated from 1 to 5, and SUS scores were calculated as (sum of item scores − 10) × 2.5, yielding a 0–100 score. Perceived usefulness was the mean of 4 study-developed items rated on a 7-point scale and based on the Technology Acceptance Model [23]. Two satisfaction items and 1 intention-to-use item were rated on 7-point scales and reported separately. Willingness to recommend was rated with 1 item on a 0–10 scale; the Net Promoter Score was calculated separately for caregivers and SLPs as the percentage of promoters (9–10) minus the percentage of detractors (0–6) [24]. Children rated ease of use and intention to use with 1 item each on a 4-point scale.

Quantitative Process Evaluation

The quantitative process evaluation described SoundBridge implementation and face-to-face therapy exposure, drawing on the implementation component of Medical Research Council guidance [25,26]. Time-stamped SoundBridge records were used to summarize child home practice (practice days, first-attempt and retry recordings, and assigned words practiced), caregiver feedback (evaluations and praise messages), and SLP activities (task generation, recording review, and praise).

Each valid first attempt or retry counted as 1 recording. A practice day required at least 1 valid first attempt; practice days per 7 days were calculated as total practice days divided by the number of days in each child’s log observation window, multiplied by 7. Caregiver evaluation coverage was the proportion of valid recordings evaluated at least once, and SLP monitoring coverage was the proportion of valid recordings played at least once; both included first attempts and retries. For each SLP activity, weekly delivery was summarized as the percentage of child-treatment weeks with at least 1 recorded occurrence.

Face-to-face therapy exposure was summarized by the number of sessions attended and articulation-treatment minutes. Intervention fidelity was assessed descriptively using 5 SLP-reported checklist items covering articulation-practice duration, target-sound production count, caregiver-counseling duration, homework word count, and the proportion of new homework words.

Assessment and Scoring Procedures

SoundBridge use began after the baseline assessment. The planned period from T0 through T2 was 43 calendar days. A trained researcher administered the K-APP remotely while a caregiver recorded the child’s speech. The treating SLP administered and recorded the speech generalization probe in person. Caregivers and SLPs completed questionnaires online. For children’s questionnaires, caregivers read the items aloud, and children selected their own responses.

Before scoring, audio files were renamed so that their filenames did not reveal child identity or assessment time point. Two level 1 SLPs with more than 20 years of clinical experience independently scored recordings from 4 children and resolved disagreements to calibrate scoring. These children were excluded from the reliability sample. One rater was the primary scorer for the K-APP and the other for the probe; each served as the second rater for the other assessment. For each assessment, both raters independently scored T0 and T2 recordings from 6 children (20%). Consonant-level agreement, calculated from the independent ratings before consensus, was 95.1% (κ=0.83) for K-APP total PCC and 91.8% (κ=0.79) for probe target PCC. For final scoring, the raters jointly resolved judgments that remained uncertain after up to 3 playbacks.

Statistical Analysis

Analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0.2 and Python version 3.12.10. Statistical significance was set at α=.05; t tests and correlations were 2-sided.

Outcome Analyses

All 30 children were included in the longitudinal speech and process outcome analyses, with the child as the unit of analysis. Child-specific caregiver and SLP measures contributed 30 observations from 29 caregivers and 18 SLPs, respectively. For T2 usability and acceptability, caregiver responses were analyzed per child (n=30) and SLP responses per clinician (n=18). Continuous variables were summarized as means and SDs or medians and IQRs; categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages.

T0–T2 change in K-APP total PCC, the primary outcome, was assessed using a paired t test. Probe target PCC, ICS-K, caregiver- and SLP-rated child self-monitoring, and caregiver-, SLP-, and child-rated alliance were each analyzed across T0, T1, and T2 using a 1-way repeated-measures ANOVA, with Greenhouse–Geisser correction when sphericity was violated. Within each outcome, Holm adjustment was applied to the P values for the T0–T1, T1–T2, and T0–T2 comparisons. T0–T2 changes in caregiver, SLP, and child self-efficacy were assessed using paired t tests. Mean changes were reported with 95% CIs; effect sizes were partial η² for ANOVAs and Cohen dz for paired t tests.

Exploratory Analyses

Target specificity was assessed using repeated-measures ANOVAs of PCC by time and phoneme status (K-APP: 2×2; probe: 3×2). The 2 time-by-status interaction P values were Holm-adjusted. Changes in target consonant accuracy across linguistic units were assessed using a 3×3 repeated-measures ANOVA of probe target PCC by time and unit (word, phrase, or sentence).

Exploratory Spearman correlations assessed associations of T0–T2 changes in K-APP total PCC and probe target PCC with 14 child-level indicators, yielding 28 tests. Five indicators were changes in caregiver- and SLP-rated child self-monitoring and caregiver-, SLP-, and child-rated alliance. The other 9 comprised 8 SoundBridge use measures—practice days, first-attempt recordings, caregiver evaluation count and coverage, SLP-reviewed unique recordings and monitoring coverage, and caregiver and SLP praise-message counts—and attended face-to-face therapy sessions, representing face-to-face therapy exposure. Spearman coefficients were reported with Bonett–Wright 95% CIs. Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate adjustment was applied separately to the 5 process and 9 platform use or face-to-face therapy exposure correlations for each outcome, yielding 4 correction families.

Additional exploratory analyses examined 7 self-monitoring subdomains and 3 alliance subscales, with Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment applied within each set separately by informant.

Sensitivity Analyses

The K-APP and probe analyses were repeated in a restricted sample of 25 children who averaged at least 2 practice days per 7 days and whose intervention duration did not exceed 46 days (the planned 43 days plus a 3-day postintervention assessment window). In the restricted-sample probe analysis, the 3 pairwise P values were Holm-adjusted as in the full sample. Estimates and 95% CIs were compared with those from the full sample.

Post hoc repeated-measures ANCOVAs examined each speech outcome with 1 mean-centered covariate at a time: the baseline value of the other speech outcome, age at T0, K-CPM IQ, or the number of face-to-face therapy sessions attended. This yielded 4 models per outcome.

To assess sensitivity to clustering by SLP, percentile 95% CIs for mean T0–T2 changes and the main Spearman correlation coefficients were estimated from 10,000 bootstrap samples of the 18 SLPs. SLPs were sampled with replacement, and all children treated by each selected SLP were retained.

Ethical Considerations

The study was approved by the Yonsei University Institutional Review Board on February 3, 2026 (7001988-202602-HR-3059-03). Caregivers and SLPs provided written informed consent; caregivers also provided written permission for their children, who gave verbal assent after a caregiver explained the study. Identifying information was stored separately from research data, which only authorized research staff could access. Caregivers received KRW 100,000, and SLPs received KRW 100,000 per participating child; participants who completed a postintervention interview received an additional KRW 20,000.
